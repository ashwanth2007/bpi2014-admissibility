\subsection{The spine log: BPI Challenge 2014}
\label{sec:spine-log}

BPI Challenge 2014 \citep{dongen2014challenge} is the incident-management record of Rabobank
Nederland Group ICT, released for the 2014 Business Process Intelligence Challenge. It is one
of the few public logs in which the assignment decision, the resource that received it and the
eventual outcome are all recorded on real work rather than simulated. Three files are used: an
incident header, an activity log and an interaction log. Table~\ref{tab:dataset} reports the
working statistics.

Five properties of the raw files silently corrupt a naive load, and they are recorded here
because they cost real debugging time and because a reader attempting to reproduce this work
will meet all five. The files are semicolon delimited rather than comma delimited. They are
encoded latin-1 rather than UTF-8, so the group names carry mojibake under a UTF-8 read. The
handle-time column uses a decimal comma, so a direct numeric cast silently produces nulls.
The incident header pads to 78 columns with empty trailing delimiters, which shifts the
column index of every field a naive parser reads positionally.

And the fifth, which is the one this work got wrong rather than avoided: the timestamps are
day-first, and the two files do not even agree on the separator. The incident header writes
\texttt{dd/mm/yyyy} and the activity log writes \texttt{dd-mm-yyyy}. On the \SI{41.0}{\percent} of
rows where both leading fields are at most twelve the date is ambiguous, a month-first parse
transposes day and month silently, and no error is raised anywhere. An earlier version of this
pipeline did exactly that, and Section~\ref{sec:harness-defects} records what it cost. The format
must be stated explicitly and never inferred: \texttt{dayfirst=True} alone is a hint that pandas is
free to ignore per row, while an explicit format string is not.


\subsection{Four further logs, and the specification fixed for each}
\label{sec:four-logs}

A protocol measured on one log says what that log costs, not what the protocol costs. Four
further public event logs are therefore carried through the identical pipeline: BPI
Challenge 2017 \citep{dongen2017challenge}, consumer lending at a Dutch financial
institute; Sepsis Cases \citep{mannhardt2016sepsis}, emergency care pathways; Hospital
Billing \citep{mannhardt2017billing}, the financial settlement of treatment episodes; and
Road Traffic Fine Management \citep{deleoni2015roadfines}, an Italian municipal penalty
process. They were chosen to span domains rather than to resemble the first, and two of them
are keyed by an individual resource rather than by a team, which is the case the single-log
study could not reach.

The risk in adding logs is not technical, it is that a decision point or a label gets chosen
after seeing what it scores. Table~\ref{tab:logspec} is the guard against that. For every
log the decision point, the outcome and the Rule 2 encoding key were written into
\texttt{code/log\_schemas.py} and committed before any score was computed on that log, and
the table is printed from that file rather than transcribed from it. Every log's number is
reported whichever way it falls; only the framing was left open.

One scoping statement belongs here rather than later. The two scoring formulations of
Section~\ref{sec:formulation-results} and the assignment study of
Section~\ref{sec:assignment-results} are built on BPI 2014 alone. They need a candidate set,
a capacity model and a per-priority service target, and only the spine log carries all three.
The five-log result is the admissibility measurement; the single-log result is what an
admissible model is then used for. They are reported separately and should not be read as one
claim.

\subsection{Two working sets, and why they differ}
\label{sec:two-sets}

The paper has two components with different data requirements, and they operate on different
subsets of the same log. Reporting one figure for both would be wrong, so both are stated.

The \emph{predictive} component uses every incident with a resolvable handle time and a
recorded priority, which is \num{46605} incidents across \num{180} distinct first-assignment
groups. It places no constraint on group size, because a target encoding with a smoothing prior
handles a rarely used group correctly by shrinking it towards the population rate.

The \emph{assignment} component of Section~\ref{sec:assignment-results} simulates a decision
over a candidate set, and a candidate set containing groups that have handled three incidents
in three years is not a realistic decision. It therefore restricts to assignment groups with at
least \num{50} recorded cases, which leaves \num{39449} incidents, \num{87} groups and
\num{61} configuration-item categories, of which the earliest \num{31559} by open time form the
training period on which every parameter is fitted.

\subsection{The service level label}
\label{sec:label}

The log's own \texttt{Alert Status} field reads \texttt{closed} on \num{46606} of the
\num{46809} raw records and carries no usable breach signal, so no contractual service level
target is recoverable from the data. The label is therefore constructed, and the construction
is stated precisely rather than described loosely.

Let $H_i$ be the recorded handle time of incident $i$ in hours and $\pi(i)$ its priority class.
Write $\tilde{H}_{p}$ for the median handle time of priority class $p$. An incident breaches
when
\begin{equation}
y_i \;=\; \mathbb{1}\bigl[\, H_i \;>\; \tau_{\pi(i)} \,\bigr],
\qquad \tau_{p} \;=\; 2\,\tilde{H}_{p}.
\label{eq:label}
\end{equation}

The factor of two is a deliberate choice of a lenient target: at a factor of one the positive
class would be half the data by construction and the label would carry no information beyond
the median. Table~\ref{tab:priority} gives the resulting thresholds. The breach rate is
notably flat across the priority classes that carry real volume, between \SI{32.4}{\percent} and
\SI{37.7}{\percent}. Priority 1 is excluded from that range because it holds \num{3} incidents in the whole log, of which \num{0} breach. The flatness is a direct consequence of defining the threshold within each class: the label measures
whether a case was slow \emph{for its own priority}, not whether it was slow in absolute terms.

\paragraph{Where the threshold is estimated, and where that matters.}
For the random-split arms, $\tilde{H}_p$ is computed over the whole log. This is a property of
the \emph{label}, not of a feature: no model observes $\tilde{H}_p$, and every model is scored
against the same fixed label, so the comparison between configurations is unaffected. For the
chronological arm it would nevertheless be wrong, because a system deployed in January 2014
cannot know the 2014 medians. That arm therefore re-derives $\tau_p$ from training-period cases
alone, and the effect is reported in Section~\ref{sec:temporal}.

\subsection{The protocol}
\label{sec:protocol}

Let $t^{\mathrm{dec}}_i$ be the moment the assignment decision for case $i$ is taken, and let
$\mathcal{F}_{t}$ be the information available to the organisation at time $t$.

\paragraph{Rule 1, feature admissibility.} An attribute $x^{(k)}$ is admissible for case $i$
only if its value is $\mathcal{F}_{t^{\mathrm{dec}}_i}$-measurable:
\begin{equation}
x^{(k)}_i \in \mathcal{F}_{t^{\mathrm{dec}}_i}
\quad\text{for every case } i .
\label{eq:rule1}
\end{equation}
Any attribute aggregated over the whole closed case violates this by construction, whatever its
apparent predictive value. This is \citeauthor{kaufman2012leakage}'s legitimacy condition
\citep{kaufman2012leakage} instantiated at a specific decision point rather than stated in
general.

\paragraph{Rule 2, encoding admissibility.} A group-level target encoding for group $g$
evaluated for case $i$ may use only cases resolved strictly before case $i$ opened:
\begin{equation}
\hat{\theta}_g(i) \;=\;
\frac{\sum_{j \in \mathcal{P}(i,g)} y_j \;+\; \alpha \bar{y}_{\mathcal{P}}}
     {\lvert \mathcal{P}(i,g) \rvert \;+\; \alpha},
\label{eq:rule2}
\end{equation}
where the admissible past set is
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{P}(i,g) = \bigl\{\, j : g_j = g,\; t^{\mathrm{res}}_j < t^{\mathrm{open}}_i \,\bigr\},
\label{eq:pastset}
\end{equation}
with smoothing prior $\alpha = 20$ and $\bar{y}_{\mathcal{P}}$ the breach rate over the
admissible past across all groups at the same instant.


\begin{figure}[htbp]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.96\textwidth]{fig19_admissibility_mechanism.pdf}
\caption{The two rules on a single case timeline. Everything left of $t^{\mathrm{dec}}$ is
\emph{observable} and admissible under Rule~\ref{eq:rule1}; everything right of it is recorded by the
log but is not knowable when the decision is taken, and it is the attributes in that region
that carry the signal this paper prices. Rule~\ref{eq:rule2} cuts a second window, over other
cases rather than over this one: the group encoding for case $i$ may average only cases that
had already \emph{resolved} when case $i$ opened, which is a stricter condition than having
started earlier. The distinction between the two shaded regions is the entire contribution;
the rest of the paper measures what enforcing it costs.}
\label{fig:mechanism}
\end{figure}

The encoding is therefore a function of a case's own history and of nothing else. It does
not depend on which rows fall in a training split, so it does not need to be refitted
inside folds: there is no train and test boundary for it to leak across. That is a
stronger guarantee than fold refitting, which only prevents an encoding from seeing the
rows in its own evaluation fold and still lets a case be encoded using cases that resolved
after it opened.

We report what that distinction is worth, because an earlier version of this work used the
weaker construction. Fitting the encoding over the training rows rather than over
$\mathcal{P}(i,g)$ raises held-out AUC by \num{0.0040} on the strongest learner. That gain is
not earned: under a stratified random split it comes from encoding a case opened in 2012
with cases that resolved in 2014. Every number reported in this paper uses
Equation~\ref{eq:rule2} as written.
\citet{pargent2022regularized} show that regularised target encoding outperforms conventional
categorical handling; the regularisation is exactly what makes refitting necessary, because an
encoding fitted once over everything contains each row's own label inside its own predictor.

Applying both rules to BPI 2014 admits \num{17} attributes and excludes nine.
Those nine are not alike, and the distinction matters for reading the cost we report.
\num{5} of them are rebuilt by the pipeline and are what the unconstrained arm actually
trains on: reassignment count, groups touched, total activity events, the reopen flag and
the related-incident count. The remaining \num{4} are recorded exclusions rather than
measured ones. They appeared in the prior pipeline this study reproduces, they are
inadmissible for the same reason, and they are listed so the protocol is auditable, but no
configuration in this paper computes them. The cost of admissibility reported in
Section~\ref{sec:cost} is therefore the cost of removing the five measured attributes, not
of removing all nine.
